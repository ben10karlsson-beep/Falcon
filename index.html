The math behind the charts

Every color in the range grid comes from a few simple ideas. Learn these and the charts stop being something to memorize and start making sense.

Equity and pot odds

Equity is your share of the pot if all the remaining cards were dealt right now. If you win 30% of the time, you own 30% of the pot on average.

Pot odds tell you how much equity you need to call. Divide what you have to call by the size of the pot after you call.

equity needed = call ÷ (pot + bet + call)

The same bet tells the bettor something too. A pure bluff only needs to work a certain share of the time to break even, and the defender has to continue often enough to stop that. That share is the minimum defense frequency (MDF).

The button opens to 2.5bb. The pot is 4bb and the big blind calls 1.5bb more, so it needs only 1.5 ÷ 5.5 = 27% equity. That cheap price is why the BB defends so many hands.

Counting outs

An out is a card that should give you the best hand. A quick estimate is the rule of 4 and 2: on the flop, multiply your outs by 4 to estimate your chance of hitting by the river. On the turn, multiply by 2.

turn to river (1 card)

The rule is close for small draws but runs high for big ones. With 15 outs it says 60%, but the real chance is about 54%.

Combinations and blockers

There are 1,326 possible two-card hands, grouped into the 169 squares of the grid. Each square holds a different number of combinations:

pair = 6 combos · suited = 4 combos · offsuit = 12 combos

So AKo is three times as common as AKs, and any unpaired hand like AK (16 combos) is more likely than any specific pair (6 combos). That is why the offsuit corner of the grid has a big effect on how wide a range really is.

Cards in your hand also block combos for your opponent. If you hold an ace, there are fewer aces left for them.

Example: why A5s is a 3-bet bluff

Holding the A♠ drops your opponent's AA combos from 6 to 3 and their AK combos from 16 to 12. The hands most likely to 4-bet you become much rarer, and A5s can still make the nut flush or a wheel straight when called.

Why position matters

Acting last means you see what your opponent does before you decide. You can take free cards, keep pots small with medium hands, and bluff when they show weakness. Out of position you guess first on every street.

What GTO means

A game-theory optimal (GTO) strategy is one that cannot be exploited: if you play it, no opposing strategy beats you in the long run. Solvers find an approximation by playing against themselves billions of times and adjusting until neither side can improve.

When two actions earn the same amount, a solver often splits between them. Those are the mixed squares in the grid. Mixing keeps your range balanced so opponents cannot tell what you hold from what you do.

Exploitative play means deviating on purpose to punish an opponent's mistakes. If someone folds too often, you bluff more. Exploits win more against weak players but leave you open to counter-exploitation, so GTO works best as the baseline you adjust from.

Range
All the hands a player could have in a spot, rather than one specific hand.
Combo
One specific two-card holding, such as A♠K♥.
Equity
Your share of the pot based on how often you would win if all cards were dealt.
EV
Expected value. The average amount an action wins or loses over the long run.
Blocker
A card in your hand that removes combos from your opponent's range.
MDF

Squeeze
A 3-bet after one player opens and another calls.

Entering the pot by just calling the big blind instead of raising.

Getting good

Falcon covers the fundamentals. These are the best outside resources for going further, in roughly the order they become useful.

A study plan

Most players improve fastest by mastering one layer at a time. Each stage builds on the one before it.

  1. Preflop (weeks 1–2)Learn the opening charts seat by seat. Play the Trainer for 15 minutes a day until you score above 90%, then switch to "My leaks".
  2. The math (weeks 3–4)Work through the Theory page and play the Equity quiz daily. Aim for an average error under 6 points and no call-or-fold mistakes.
  3. Flop strategy (weeks 5–8)Use the Board explorer on many textures. Before you click, predict who has the range and nut advantage, then check yourself.
  4. Solver study (ongoing)Pick one spot per session, such as BTN vs BB on a K-high flop, and study it in a solver. Write down one rule you learned.
  5. Review your own hands (ongoing)After each session, mark two or three tricky hands and run them through the equity calculator. Your own mistakes are your best study material.

Good habits

  • Study more than you play at first. Early on, aim for about one hour of study for every two of play.
  • Fix one leak at a time. Pick the biggest one, from the Trainer or your hand reviews, and focus on it for a week.
  • Manage your bankroll. A common guideline for cash games is at least 20–30 buy-ins for the stakes you play. Move down when you drop below that.
  • Judge decisions, not results. A good call that loses is still a good call. The math works over thousands of hands, not one.
  • Play within your limits. Only play with money you can afford to lose, and set time and loss limits. If poker stops being fun or starts costing more than you planned, contact your local gambling support service.
