The math behind the charts

Every color in the range grid comes from a few simple ideas. Learn these and the charts stop being something to memorize and start making sense.

Equity and pot odds

Equity is your share of the pot if all the remaining cards were dealt right now. If you win 30% of the time, you own 30% of the pot on average.

Pot odds tell you how much equity you need to call. Divide what you have to call by the size of the pot after you call.

equity needed = call ÷ (pot + bet + call)

The same bet tells the bettor something too. A pure bluff only needs to work a certain share of the time to break even, and the defender has to continue often enough to stop that. That share is the minimum defense frequency (MDF).

The button opens to 2.5bb. The pot is 4bb and the big blind calls 1.5bb more, so it needs only 1.5 ÷ 5.5 = 27% equity. That cheap price is why the BB defends so many hands.

Counting outs

An out is a card that should give you the best hand. A quick estimate is the rule of 4 and 2: on the flop, multiply your outs by 4 to estimate your chance of hitting by the river. On the turn, multiply by 2.

turn to river (1 card)
rule of 4 and 2 estimate

The rule is close for small draws but runs high for big ones. With 15 outs it says 60%, but the real chance is about 54%.

Combinations and blockers

A combo is one exact pair of cards, suits included. A♠K♥ and A♦K♣ are both "AK", but they are two different combos. Combos matter because they tell you how often a hand gets dealt.

Each square in the grid holds a fixed number of combos, depending on its type:

TypeEvery comboCount
Pair: AAA♠A♥ · A♠A♦ · A♠A♣ · A♥A♦ · A♥A♣ · A♦A♣6
Suited: AKsA♠K♠ · A♥K♥ · A♦K♦ · A♣K♣4
Offsuit: AKo4 aces × 4 kings = 16 ways, minus the 4 suited ones12

All 169 squares add up to 1,326 combos, which is every possible starting hand. Two things follow:

  • Offsuit hands are common. AKo gets dealt three times as often as AKs. Adding a few offsuit squares to a range widens it far more than adding the same number of suited squares.
  • Unpaired hands get dealt more than pairs. AK (16 combos) gets dealt almost three times as often as AA (6 combos). When someone could have either, they have AK more often.

Blockers: your cards change the count

There are only four of each rank in the deck. Every card you hold is a card your opponent can't have, so it blocks some of their combos. You can count what's left with two rules:

Example: why A5s is a 3-bet bluff

Holding the A♠ drops your opponent's AA combos from 6 to 3 and their AK combos from 16 to 12. The hands most likely to 4-bet you become much rarer, and A5s can still make the nut flush or a wheel straight when called.

Equity against a range

At the table you never see the other player's cards. So instead of asking "what's my equity against their hand?", you ask "what's my equity against every hand they could have?" That list of hands is their range, and it comes from what you know: their seat, their actions, and how they play.

You work it out in four steps:

  1. Put them on a range. Which hands would this player take this action with?
  2. Count the combos of each hand, minus the ones your cards block.
  3. Find your equity against each hand. The matchup patterns above get you close.
  4. Average them, weighted by how often they have each hand. If half their combos are AK, then half the time you're up against AK, so AK makes up half the answer.
your equity = add up (how often they have the hand × your equity against it)

"How often" is that hand's combos divided by the total combos in their range. A hand they play only some of the time counts for part of its combos: AJo played half the time is 6 combos instead of 12.

Worked example: A♠Q♥ against a tight 3-bet

A tight player 3-bets and you put them on QQ+ and AK. Your ace and queen block some of those combos, so count those first. Then weight each hand by how often they have it:

Your equity is about 24%. The biggest part of that comes from AK, not AA. AK is half their range and it dominates you, while AA is only one combo in eight because your ace blocks it.

Now say this player 3-bets wider and also has JJ, TT, AQs and AJs. That adds 17 combos, and you do much better against them: about 43% against JJ and TT and 70% against AJs. Your equity climbs to 34.3%. Your hand is the same; only their range changed. (AQs adds only 2 combos, because your A♠ and Q♥ block the other two.)

Using it: do you call the all-in?

The button opens, you 3-bet A♠Q♥ to 11bb from the small blind, and the button moves all in for 100bb. The pot is 112bb (your 11, the big blind's 1, their 100) and you need 89bb more to call. By the pot odds formula you need 89 ÷ 201 = 44.3% equity.

A tight player shoves about QQ+ and AK here, which gives you 24%. Even the wider range only gives you 34%. Both are well short of 44%, so this is a fold, even though AQo is a strong hand. All-in spots are where this math is most exact: no more betting is left, so your equity is exactly the share of the pot you win on average.

At the table, without a calculator

Mid-hand you don't need the exact number. You only need to know one thing: is my equity above or below what my pot odds ask for? A rough estimate answers that, and you can make one in four steps.

1
Sort their range into three groups. Use the cheat sheet below. Each group gets one rough equity, so you don't have to remember exact matchups.
2
Count the combos in each group. Use 6 / 4 / 12, and take off what your cards block.
3
Multiply each group's combos by its rough equity.
4
Add the results and divide by the total combos. That's your estimate. Compare it to your pot odds.

When both hands are unknown

The same idea works when neither hand is known, for example when you study how two whole ranges do against each other. You average over both sides: every hand in one range against every hand in the other. Before the flop, the button's opening range has about 54% equity against the hands the big blind calls with. That's close to even because the big blind folds its worst hands and keeps the ones that play well. The board explorer on the Postflop tab shows how that changes on each flop.

What to take from this

  • Count combos, don't just list hands. The hands that make up most of their range decide your equity. One scary hand with few combos matters less than it feels.
  • Every action narrows the range. An open, a 3-bet and a 4-bet each remove weaker hands, so the same hand is worth less as the betting goes up. AJo has 60% against a button open but about 51% against an UTG open. Try both in the calculator.
  • Compare it to your pot odds. Range equity is the number you check against the equity you need to call. If it's lower, fold.
  • Before the river, equity isn't everything. If more betting is still to come, position and how well your hand plays after the flop decide how much of your equity you actually win.

Expected value: is the call worth it?

Equity tells you how often you win. Expected value (EV) turns that into what you care about: how many big blinds a decision wins or loses on average, if you made it many times.

When you call, two things can happen:

  • You win (as often as your equity): you gain everything already in the pot.
  • You lose (the rest of the time): you lose the chips you called with.
EV of calling = equity × pot − (1 − equity) × call

Folding is always 0. The chips you put in earlier already belong to the pot, not to you, so folding doesn't lose them again. Only what happens from here on counts. That's why "I've already put in so much" is never a reason to call.

Example: the AQo all-in, in big blinds

From the range section: the pot is 112bb, you need 89bb to call, and you have 24.4% against a tight shoving range.

Every time you make this call, you lose 40bb on average. Make it 100 times and that's about 4,000bb, or 40 full buy-ins. Folding costs nothing from here, so folding is better by 40bb.

Against the wider range (34.3%) the call still loses about 20bb. At exactly 44.3% the two outcomes cancel out and EV is 0. That's the same number the pot odds formula gave you: pot odds are just the equity where calling breaks even.

Treat it as a starting point there, not the final answer.

Why position matters

Acting last means you see what your opponent does before you decide. You can take free cards, keep pots small with medium hands, and bluff when they show weakness. Out of position you guess first on every street.

What GTO means

A game-theory optimal (GTO) strategy is one that cannot be exploited: if you play it, no opposing strategy beats you in the long run. Solvers find an approximation by playing against themselves billions of times and adjusting until neither side can improve.

When two actions earn the same amount, a solver often splits between them. Those are the mixed squares in the grid. Mixing keeps your range balanced so opponents cannot tell what you hold from what you do.

Exploitative play means deviating on purpose to punish an opponent's mistakes. If someone folds too often, you bluff more. Exploits win more against weak players but leave you open to counter-exploitation, so GTO works best as the baseline you adjust from.

Glossary

RFI
Raise first in. Opening the pot with a raise when everyone before you folded.
3-bet
A re-raise of an open raise. A raise of that is a 4-bet.
IP / OOP
In position (acting last after the flop) or out of position (acting first).
Range
All the hands a player could have in a spot, rather than one specific hand.
Range equity
Your equity against every hand an opponent could have, weighted by how many combos of each there are.
Combo
One specific two-card holding, such as A♠K♥.
Equity
Your share of the pot based on how often you would win if all cards were dealt.
EV
Expected value. The average amount an action wins or loses over the long run.
Blocker
A card in your hand that removes combos from your opponent's range.
MDF
Minimum defense frequency. How often you must continue so bluffs with any two cards do not profit.
Squeeze
A 3-bet after one player opens and another calls.

Entering the pot by just calling the big blind instead of raising.

Getting good

Falcon covers the fundamentals. These are the best outside resources for going further, in roughly the order they become useful.

A study plan

Most players improve fastest by mastering one layer at a time. Each stage builds on the one before it.

  1. Preflop (weeks 1–2)Learn the opening charts seat by seat. Play the Trainer for 15 minutes a day until you score above 90%, then switch to "My leaks".
  2. The math (weeks 3–4)Work through the Theory page and play the Equity quiz daily. Aim for an average error under 6 points and no call-or-fold mistakes.
  3. Flop strategy (weeks 5–8)Use the Board explorer on many textures. Before you click, predict who has the range and nut advantage, then check yourself.
  4. Solver study (ongoing)Pick one spot per session, such as BTN vs BB on a K-high flop, and study it in a solver. Write down one rule you learned.
  5. Review your own hands (ongoing)After each session, mark two or three tricky hands and run them through the equity calculator. Your own mistakes are your best study material.

Good habits

  • Study more than you play at first. Early on, aim for about one hour of study for every two of play.
  • Fix one leak at a time. Pick the biggest one, from the Trainer or your hand reviews, and focus on it for a week.
  • Manage your bankroll. A common guideline for cash games is at least 20–30 buy-ins for the stakes you play. Move down when you drop below that.
  • Judge decisions, not results. A good call that loses is still a good call. The math works over thousands of hands, not one.
  • Play within your limits. Only play with money you can afford to lose, and set time and loss limits. If poker stops being fun or starts costing more than you planned, contact your local gambling support service.
