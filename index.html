Against one hand Against a range Strategy

The math behind the charts

Seven short chapters, meant to be read in order. Each one only uses ideas from the chapters before it. Chapters 1–3 work against a hand you can see. Chapters 4–6 do the same against a whole range, which is what you face at a real table. Chapter 7 shows how it all shapes the charts.

Chapter 1 of 7

Equity: how often you win

Builds on: nothing. Start here.

Equity is your share of the pot if all the remaining cards were dealt right now. If you would win 30% of the time, you own 30% of the pot on average. A tie counts as half a win.

Equity is the number everything else in this page is built on. Chapters 3 and 6 compare it with the price of a call, and chapter 5 averages it over all the hands your opponent could have.

No hand is a lock before the flop. These all-in equities show the patterns worth knowing:

MatchupEquityPattern

Two lessons stand out. Domination is brutal: sharing a card with a better kicker leaves you around 26%. And a pair against two overcards is roughly a coin flip, which is why small pairs and big suited cards both have value.

Equity changes with every card. After the flop it can swing hard: a coin flip before the flop can turn into a big favourite, or a hand that is nearly dead. Chapter 2 shows how to estimate it once there's a board.

Recap
  • Equity is how often you'd win if all the cards were dealt, so it's your share of the pot on average.
  • Being dominated (same card, worse kicker) is the worst common spot: about 26%.
  • Pair vs two overcards is close to a flip. Big favourites are pair over pair (about 82%) and dominating hands.

Chapter 2 of 7

Outs: your equity when you're behind

Builds on: 1. Equity

After the flop, when you're behind, your equity comes from the cards that would put you ahead. An out is a card that should give you the best hand. Count your outs and you can estimate your equity without a calculator.

The quick estimate is the rule of 4 and 2: on the flop, multiply your outs by 4 to estimate your chance of hitting by the river. On the turn, with one card to come, multiply by 2.

flop to river (2 cards)
turn to river (1 card)
rule of 4 and 2 estimate

The rule is close for small draws but runs high for big ones. With 15 outs it says 60%, but the real chance is about 54%.

Only count clean outs. Some cards improve your hand but also improve theirs. If you have a flush draw and they have a set, a flush card that pairs the board can give them a full house. Those are tainted outs: leave them out of the count, or count them as half. The equity quiz shows them separately.

Recap
  • An out is a card that should give you the best hand.
  • Outs × 4 on the flop, outs × 2 on the turn. It overestimates big draws a little.
  • Don't count cards that help you but give them an even better hand.

Chapter 3 of 7

The price: pot odds and EV

Builds on: 1. Equity · 2. Outs

Equity tells you how often you win. The price tells you how often you need to win for a call to pay off. Every call decision is just a comparison of the two.

Throughout Falcon, the pot means every chip in the middle before you call, including the bet you're facing. You risk your call to win the pot:

equity needed = call ÷ (pot + call)

That number is your pot odds, written as the equity you need. Bets are usually described as a share of the pot before the bet, so there is a shortcut for that:

equity needed = bet ÷ (pot before the bet + 2 × bet)

Both give the same answer. A half-pot bet asks for 25%, a pot-size bet for 33%. Move the slider to see others:

Example: a flush draw on the turn

You hold 9♥ 8♥ and your opponent shows K♠ Q♦. The board is K♥ 7♥ 2♣ 4♠. Nine hearts are left and each one gives you the best hand, so you have 9 clean outs (chapter 2): 9 ÷ 44 unseen cards = 20.5%.

The pot was 10bb and they bet 5bb. The pot is now 15bb and you must call 5bb, so you need 5 ÷ (15 + 5) = 25%. You have 20.5%: fold. If they had bet 2.5bb instead, you'd need 2.5 ÷ 15 = 16.7%, and calling would be right.

Expected value: how much a call is worth

Expected value (EV) turns the comparison into big blinds: how much a decision wins or loses on average, if you made it many times. When you call, two things can happen. You win the pot as often as your equity, and you lose your call the rest of the time:

EV of calling = equity × pot − (1 − equity) × call
The flush draw vs 5bbHow oftenResultCounts for
You hit20.5%+15bb0.205 × 15 = +3.07bb
You miss79.5%−5bb0.795 × 5 = −3.98bb
EV of calling−0.91bb

Each time you make this call you lose 0.91bb on average. Against the 2.5bb bet the same draw is worth +0.57bb. With exactly 25% equity against the 5bb bet, the two lines cancel out (0.25 × 15 = 0.75 × 5) and EV is 0. That's the link between the two ideas: pot odds are the equity where calling breaks even.

Folding is always 0. The chips you put in earlier already belong to the pot, so folding doesn't lose them again. Only what happens from here on counts. That's why "I've already put in so much" is never a reason to call.

In the charts: why the big blind defends so much

The button opens to 2.5bb. With the blinds, the pot is 4bb and the big blind has to call 1.5bb, so it needs only 1.5 ÷ (4 + 1.5) = 27% equity. That cheap price is why the BB defends so many hands.

When the math is exact. It's exact when you're all in or on the river, because no more betting can happen. With chips still behind it's an estimate: later bets can win you more when you hit, or cost you more when you miss. Treat it as the starting point there, not the final answer.

Recap
  • Equity needed = call ÷ (pot + call), where the pot includes their bet.
  • Call when your equity is above the price, fold when it's below.
  • EV says by how much: pot odds are the break-even point where EV is 0.

Chapter 4 of 7

Combos and blockers

Builds on: nothing new, it's just counting. Chapter 5 uses it to weigh your equity.

At a real table you never see the other player's cards. You think about every hand they could have, their range. But a range isn't a list where every hand counts the same: some hands get dealt far more often than others. To weigh them you count combos.

A combo is one exact pair of cards, suits included. A♠K♥ and A♦K♣ are both "AK", but they are two different combos. You never count combos one at a time: you learn three numbers, count ranges in chunks, and adjust for the cards you can see.

Three numbers, and why they're true

If you know where a number comes from, you can rebuild it when blockers change it.

HandCombosWhy
Pair: AA64 aces, and every two of them make a pair: A♠A♥ · A♠A♦ · A♠A♣ · A♥A♦ · A♥A♣ · A♦A♣
Suited: AKs4One for each suit
Offsuit: AKo124 aces × 4 kings = 16 ways, minus the 4 suited ones
Both: AK164 × 4. A hand written without s or o means both, so all 16

All 169 squares of the grid add up to 1,326 combos, every possible starting hand. Two things follow:

  • Offsuit hands are common. AKo gets dealt three times as often as AKs. Adding a few offsuit squares to a range widens it far more than adding the same number of suited squares.
  • Unpaired hands get dealt more than pairs. AK (16 combos) gets dealt almost three times as often as AA (6 combos). When someone could have either, they have AK more often.

Count in chunks, not hand by hand

Ranges are written in runs like "TT+" or "ATs+". Count the squares in the run, then multiply by 6, 4, 12 or 16.

RangeSquaresCombos
QQ+QQ, KK, AA3 × 6 = 18
TT+5 pairs5 × 6 = 30
22+every pair, 13 of them13 × 6 = 78
ATs+AKs, AQs, AJs, ATs4 × 4 = 16
A2s+every suited ace, 12 of them12 × 4 = 48
AJo+AKo, AQo, AJo3 × 12 = 36
AQ+AK and AQ, suited and offsuit2 × 16 = 32
JTs–54sJTs, T9s, 98s, 87s, 76s, 65s, 54s7 × 4 = 28

Then add the chunks. "QQ+, AK" is 18 + 16 = 34 combos. "TT+, AQ+, AJs, KQs" is 30 + 32 + 4 + 4 = 70. A hand played only part of the time counts for part of its combos: AJo played half the time is 6 combos instead of 12.

For a wide range, work from its size instead: every 1% of hands is about 13 combos, because there are 1,326 in total. Falcon's UTG open is 17% of hands, about 230 combos. The button open is 45%, about 600. You rarely need the total, but it tells you how much one hand matters: AA is 6 of UTG's 230 combos, under 3% of the range.

Blockers: adjust for the cards you can see

There are only four of each rank. Every card you can see is one they can't hold, so it blocks some of their combos: your two cards, and after the flop the board cards too. For each rank in the hand, count how many are left, then:

HandRuleCommon results
PairPairs you can make from the cards left4 left → 6 · 3 left → 3 · 2 left → 1 · 1 left → 0
Unpaired, bothCards left of one rank × cards left of the other4 × 4 = 16 · 3 × 4 = 12 · 3 × 3 = 9 · 2 × 4 = 8
SuitedStart at 4 and take one off for each suit where either card is goneYou hold A♠: AKs → 3
OffsuitUnpaired total minus the suited onesYou hold A♠: AKo → 12 − 3 = 9

The short version to remember: the first card you see of a rank cuts its pair in half (6 → 3) and takes a quarter off each unpaired hand with that rank (16 → 12).

Example: why A5s is a 3-bet bluff

Holding the A♠ drops your opponent's AA combos from 6 to 3 and their AK combos from 16 to 12. The hands most likely to 4-bet you become much rarer, and A5s can still make the nut flush or a wheel straight when called.

After the flop

The board blocks too, and a few counts come up again and again. On a flop like K♦ 7♠ 2♣, with none of those ranks in your hand:

They haveExampleCombos
A set77: three sevens are left3
Two pair with both board ranksK7: 3 kings × 3 sevens9 only 2 if suited: ♥ and ♣ (the board has the K♦ and 7♠)
Top pair, one kickerAK: 3 kings × 4 aces12
An overpairAA: no aces on the board6

So a set is 3 combos, but one top-pair hand is 12. That's why most ranges hold far more top pairs than sets, even though it feels like your opponent always has the set.

Flush draws are easy to count too. On a two-tone flop like K♥ 7♥ 2♣, each suited hand with neither card on the board has exactly one flush-draw combo, the hearts one. So the number of flush-draw combos in their range equals the number of suited hands in it.

You hold A♥ K♣. You put your opponent on AA, KK, QQ, JJ, 77, 22, KQ and KJ. Count what's left of each, taking off your cards and the board:

  • Pair 6, suited 4, offsuit 12, both 16. Count ranges in chunks: QQ+ is 3 × 6 = 18.
  • For each rank, count the cards left. Pairs: 4 → 6, 3 → 3, 2 → 1. Unpaired: left × left.

Chapter 5 of 7

Equity against a range

Builds on: 1. Equity · 4. Combos and blockers

Chapter 1 gave you your equity against one hand. At the table you ask a different question: what's my equity against every hand they could have? The answer is an average of chapter 1 equities, weighted by the combos from chapter 4.

  1. Put them on a range. Which hands would this player take this action with? Their seat and their actions tell you.
  2. Count the combos of each hand, minus the ones your cards block (chapter 4).
  3. Find your equity against each hand. The matchup patterns from chapter 1 get you close.
  4. Average them, weighted by how often they have each hand. If half their combos are AK, then half the time you're up against AK, so AK makes up half the answer.
your equity = add up (how often they have the hand × your equity against it)

"How often" is that hand's combos divided by the total combos in their range.

Worked example: A♠Q♥ against a tight 3-bet

A tight player 3-bets and you put them on QQ+ and AK. Your ace and queen block some of those combos, so count those first. Then weight each hand by how often they have it:

Their handCombos leftHow oftenYour equityAdds
AA3 you hold an ace3 ÷ 24 = 12.5%7.3%0.9%
KK66 ÷ 24 = 25%28.3%7.1%
QQ3 you hold a queen3 ÷ 24 = 12.5%30.4%3.8%
AK12 you hold an ace12 ÷ 24 = 50%25.3%12.6%
Total24100%24.4%

Your equity is about 24%. The biggest part of that comes from AK, not AA. AK is half their range and it dominates you, while AA is only one combo in eight because your ace blocks it.

Now say this player 3-bets wider and also has JJ, TT, AQs and AJs. That adds 17 combos, and you do much better against them: about 43% against JJ and TT and 70% against AJs. Your equity climbs to 34.3%. Your hand is the same; only their range changed. (AQs adds only 2 combos, because your A♠ and Q♥ block the other two.)

After the flop: back to K♦ 7♠ 2♣

In chapter 4 you held A♥ K♣ and counted their range: 10 combos beat you and 28 don't. After the flop the groups are simpler. Against a better made hand you need running cards, so use about 5%. Against a worse pair they have few outs, so use about 90%:

(10 × 5 + 28 × 90) ÷ 38 = 2,570 ÷ 38 ≈ 68%

The exact answer is 66.3%. Against the hands that beat you, your real equity runs from 0.3% to 8.6%, and against the ones you beat it's 85% to 91%, so the rough numbers were close.

Chapter 6 of 7

Putting it together: calling against a range

Builds on: 3. Pot odds and EV · 5. Equity against a range

This is where everything joins up. Facing a bet at a real table, you answer three questions:

1
What do they have? Put them on a range and count it (chapter 4).
2
What's my equity against it? Weigh it by combos (chapter 5).
3
What's the price? Call ÷ (pot + call), then compare (chapter 3).
Example: do you call the all-in?

The button opens, you 3-bet A♠Q♥ to 11bb from the small blind, and the button moves all in for 100bb. The pot is 112bb (your 11, the big blind's 1, their 100) and you need 89bb more to call.

1. A tight player shoves about QQ+ and AK here. 2. Chapter 5 worked out your equity against that: 24.4%. 3. You need 89 ÷ (112 + 89) = 44.3%.

24% is far below 44%, so fold, even though AQo is a strong hand. In big blinds:

OutcomeHow oftenResultCounts for
You win24.4%+112bb0.244 × 112 = +27.3bb
You lose75.6%−89bb0.756 × 89 = −67.3bb
EV of calling−40.0bb

Every time you make this call, you lose 40bb on average. Make it 100 times and that's about 4,000bb, or 40 full buy-ins. Against the wider range from chapter 5 (34.3%, or about 35% in your head) the call still loses about 20bb. Only a much wider shoving range would make it a call.

All-in spots are where this math is exact: no more betting is left, so your equity is exactly the share of the pot you win on average.

When it's not all in

Most calls happen with chips still behind, like calling a c-bet on the flop. The same three questions apply, with two extra cautions:

  • Their betting range is narrower than their whole range. Players bet strong hands and bluffs more often than medium ones, so the range you face is usually stronger at the top than the range they started with.
  • You don't always get your full equity. More betting is still to come. Out of position, or with a hand that's hard to continue with on later streets, you win less of it. In position, or with a strong draw, you can win more.

When it's close, be careful. If your estimate lands within about 5% of the price, the shortcut isn't precise enough to decide for you. Go with what you know about the player, and check the spot later with the calculators.

Recap
  • Range → equity against it → price. Call when the equity is above the price.
  • All in, the math is exact. With chips behind, it's a starting point.
  • If it's within about 5%, the read on the player decides it, not the math.

Chapter 7 of 7

Ranges and strategy

Builds on: 3. Pot odds and EV · 5. Equity against a range

The first six chapters were about one decision: should I call? This one zooms out to how the same ideas shape the charts and a whole strategy.

Why position matters

Acting last means you see what your opponent does before you decide. You can take free cards, keep pots small with medium hands, and bluff when they show weakness. Out of position you guess first on every street. In chapter 6 terms: in position you win more of your equity.

The result is visible in the opening charts. The later your seat, the fewer players are left behind you and the more hands are profitable to open:

The big blind is the other end of the same idea. It defends a wide range because the price is so good (27%, from chapter 3), even though it plays out of position.

Range against range

Chapter 5 put your one hand against their range. When you study a spot, you can average over both sides instead: every hand in one range against every hand in the other. Before the flop, the button's opening range has about 54% equity against the hands the big blind calls with. That's close to even because the big blind folds its worst hands and keeps the ones that play well. The board explorer shows how that changes on each flop, and who has more of the strongest hands.

The bettor's side: bluffs and MDF

Chapter 3 looked at a bet from the caller's side. The bettor has a price too. A pure bluff risks the bet to win the pot before the bet, so it needs to work bet ÷ (pot + bet) of the time. To stop bluffs with any two cards from profiting, the defender has to continue at least the rest of the time. That's the minimum defense frequency (MDF).

Bet sizeCaller needsBluff must workMDF
⅓ pot20%25%75%
½ pot25%33.3%66.7%
¾ pot30%42.9%57.1%
Pot33.3%50%50%
1.5× pot37.5%60%40%

Bigger bets need more equity to call, but they also need bluffs to work more often. MDF is a guide, not a rule: against players who rarely bluff you can fold more, and against players who bluff too much you can call more.

What GTO means

A game-theory optimal (GTO) strategy is one that cannot be exploited: if you play it, no opposing strategy beats you in the long run. Solvers find an approximation by playing against themselves billions of times and adjusting until neither side can improve.

When two actions earn the same amount, a solver often splits between them. Those are the mixed squares in the grid. Mixing keeps your range balanced so opponents can't tell what you hold from what you do.

Exploitative play means deviating on purpose to punish an opponent's mistakes. If someone folds too often, you bluff more. Exploits win more against weak players but leave you open to counter-exploitation, so GTO works best as the baseline you adjust from.

Recap
  • Later seats open wider because fewer players are left and they play in position.
  • The bettor's price is bet ÷ (pot + bet). MDF is the rest, and it's a guide, not a rule.
  • GTO is the unexploitable baseline. Mixed squares are spots where two actions earn the same.

Getting good

Falcon covers the fundamentals. These are the best outside resources for going further, in roughly the order they become useful.

A study plan

Most players improve fastest by mastering one layer at a time. Each stage builds on the one before it.

  1. Preflop (weeks 1–2)Learn the opening charts seat by seat. Play the Trainer for 15 minutes a day until you score above 90%, then switch to "My leaks".
  2. The math (weeks 3–4)Read the Theory chapters in order, one or two a day, and do each chapter's practice before moving on. Chapters 1–3 go with the Equity quiz (aim for an average error under 6 points and no call-or-fold mistakes), 4 with the combo drill, and 5–6 with the Range trainer.
  3. Flop strategy (weeks 5–8)Use the Board explorer on many textures. Before you click, predict who has the range and nut advantage, then check yourself.
  4. Solver study (ongoing)Pick one spot per session, such as BTN vs BB on a K-high flop, and study it in a solver. Write down one rule you learned.
  5. Review your own hands (ongoing)After each session, mark two or three tricky hands and run them through the equity calculator. Your own mistakes are your best study material.

Good habits

  • Study more than you play at first. Early on, aim for about one hour of study for every two of play.
  • Fix one leak at a time. Pick the biggest one, from the Trainer or your hand reviews, and focus on it for a week.
  • Manage your bankroll. A common guideline for cash games is at least 20–30 buy-ins for the stakes you play. Move down when you drop below that.
  • Judge decisions, not results. A good call that loses is still a good call. The math works over thousands of hands, not one.
  • Play within your limits. Only play with money you can afford to lose, and set time and loss limits. If poker stops being fun or starts costing more than you planned, contact your local gambling support service.
